India already has both halves of the answer and cannot look at them together. INCOIS runs an ocean model, a computed picture of what the water is doing from the surface all the way down. India also has robot floats and anchored buoys drifting in that same water, taking real measurements. Today the model lives in one tool and the measurements live in another, and both draw flat maps, one depth at a time. So the question a cyclone forecaster actually wants answered, which is whether the model is right here, at this depth, today, has no tool behind it. The problem statement asks for a web platform that brings model output and real observations together in 3D, and we read the word "together" as the whole of the brief. The picture is not the deliverable. The comparison is.

OceanVerity draws the Indian Ocean as a solid block of water you can fly inside, from the surface down to two kilometres, with the instruments sitting in it exactly where they were on the date on screen. Click one and you see, on a single chart, what that instrument actually measured going down through the water, what the model predicted in the same place on the same day, and how far apart the two were. It opens in an ordinary browser, with nothing to install, no account, no licence and no specialist software. It makes no internet requests at all while it runs, because the data travels with the page, so a dead venue network cannot stop a demonstration and the public version needs no server behind it. The build covers the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal and the equatorial Indian Ocean, holds a full year of INCOIS analyses ten days apart, carries 19 ocean variables, and places more than 270 real instruments where they actually were.

Four things separate this from a visualisation. First, it publishes its own error. Most platforms show you the model and stop. This one compares the model against every instrument in the water and prints the answer, including where the answer is unflattering. Across all of them the model usually sits within about a quarter of a degree of what was measured, and we deliberately do not leave it there. INCOIS build their model partly from the floats themselves, so a float agreeing with the model is partly the model agreeing with data it was made from. The anchored buoys are a separate network and are the harder, fairer test, and the model does visibly worse against them. We report the two separately and refuse to average them into one better-looking number, because that number would be true and misleading at the same time.

Second, no scientific question is ever answered from the picture. The block on screen is a compressed copy made for the graphics card: the values are squeezed, the depth scale is stretched so the upper ocean gets more of the screen, and the edges are smoothed so the rendering does not tear at the coast. Each of those is something a renderer needs and a scientist must never be handed. So the system keeps two copies. The full-precision numbers answer every question a person or another program asks, and the compressed copy only ever goes to the screen. Nothing reads a value back out of the picture, ever. This matters most where the platform hands data to other software, because someone pulling a file over the network cannot see which copy they were given.

Third, a gap is drawn as a gap. Where nobody has measured, we say so instead of colouring it in. About a tenth of the block has no real observation behind it, and one variable exists purely to show where the evidence is thin, which is also a map of where the next float would be worth deploying. An interpolated field will cheerfully give you a smooth, confident and completely unsupported number in the middle of an ocean nobody has visited.

Fourth, it has a second door. Nineteen variables is right for a forecaster and wrong for anybody else, and the problem statement names students, the public and policymakers as audiences. So "Explore" turns the platform into nine plain questions, such as where a cyclone could get stronger or where something adrift would go. Each is a preset over controls that already exist, and each one that simplifies something away carries its caveat right beside it, because the reader this door exists for is exactly the reader who will not add it themselves. An exhibition mode hides the controls, enlarges the text for a room, loops the questions and resets once visitors leave, and a guided tour walks a newcomer through every control.

For disaster management, one press sets the scene up for a cyclone question. A storm's fuel is not at the sea surface; what matters is how deep the warm water runs, how far the wind has already stirred the top of the ocean, and how much warm water is stacked underneath. The platform shows those as shapes inside the block rather than as a table of numbers, so you can watch the warm layer thicken and thin across the basin. We did not simply invent those calculations, because a wrong constant in an ocean formula produces a number that is smooth, plausible and completely wrong. INCOIS published these same products for fifteen years up to 2019, so we ran our code over that archive and compared results cell by cell against theirs. Our answer matches INCOIS's own published values in about 97 per cent of cells. Where our default differs it is because we use the modern standard definition rather than the older one, and the platform says which is which instead of quietly adopting whichever matched better.

There is also a real storm in it. Cyclone Montha crossed the Bay of Bengal in late October 2025 and made landfall near Narsapur, drawn on the India Meteorological Department's own official track rather than a line we invented. The platform walks through it step by step on the dates it happened, showing the ocean lose heat along the storm's path while the same bay gained heat away from it over the same ten days. That is the cold wake, made visible rather than asserted. It also states what the case does not show, including one place where the expected change never appeared, because leaving that out would have been the easier and less honest choice.

For search and rescue, drop a pin anywhere in the water and the platform follows the currents forward and draws where that point could travel. It runs inside the browser, so it works with the network unplugged. The important part is that it publishes its own accuracy rather than assuming it is correct. We tested it against real floats, which surface, report where they are, sink and drift again, so their true movement is known. Over a ten-day cycle the predicted position is typically about 41 kilometres from where the float actually turned up, and over longer periods the error grows until it exceeds the distance travelled, which means currents alone stop telling you much about a particular object after roughly a month. Saying that is the feature. This uses currents only, with no wind, so it is a starting point for a search and never a rescue forecast, and the platform says so on screen rather than in a footnote.

The ocean matters to fishermen too. The platform shows fronts, the boundaries where different bodies of water meet and where fish gather, found by the method INCOIS name for their own advisories, and below them plankton, dissolved oxygen and the depth where oxygen gets too low for many fish. The same comparison engine scores those against the floats carrying those sensors. We never use the words "fishing zone": these are the ingredients an advisory is built from, and the advisory stays INCOIS's. On a longer view it compares this year's ocean against the thirty-year average for the same month, at every depth. And an oceanographer can draw a line anywhere and get a vertical slice along it, computed from the numbers rather than read off the picture.

Underneath, every data source is one small piece of code behind a single seam. There are eleven, including public archives from INCOIS, Argo, the India Meteorological Department, Copernicus and NOAA, plus one for an ocean data file a visitor drops onto the page, which is then drawn by the same renderer with the same controls and stored nowhere. That seam was tested rather than claimed: a later round added four new data sources and touched no renderer, no interface and no screen. The analysis also leaves the platform in the standard formats ocean portals and GIS software already read, so other tools can use it rather than only look at it.

You can believe the numbers because something breaks if they are wrong. There are over 500 automated tests covering the science, every calculated variable checked against a case worked out by hand, and 16 browser checks that measure what the page actually drew rather than trusting it. The two pieces of science that also had to run in the browser are checked against the originals and fail the build if they ever disagree. Every figure on the public provenance page is read live from the build, so it cannot claim a number the platform does not hold, and a script fails the repository if a figure typed into any document has drifted from the real one. The list of known defects is public, including the ones still open.
