# SIH submission guidance, collected

**Status: a suggestion, not an instruction.** This file is material the owner gathered from
people who have judged or submitted to SIH, plus one evaluator-psychology rubric. It is *not*
official. **Some of it is out of date** - it was written against SIH 2025 and names a 2025
deadline, a 36-hour hackathon shape and field limits that may have changed. Where this file and
sih.gov.in disagree, **sih.gov.in wins**, and the official template wins over both.

Read it as a checklist to argue with. Several points below were already wrong for this project
and were deliberately not applied; those are marked **[not applied]** with the reason, so the
next session does not re-apply them.

The two files that hold decisions rather than suggestions are [`NOTES.md`](NOTES.md), which is
how this deck is written, and [`DECK.md`](DECK.md), which is what each slide says.

---

## 1. The evaluator rubric: five criteria, 20% each

| | Problem fit | Innovation | Feasibility | Tech depth | Presentation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What judges seek** | Clear definition, impact scale, target users | Novel approaches, creative solutions, uniqueness | Technical viability, resource needs, timeline | Code quality, architecture, complexity handling | Clear communication, demo quality, storytelling |
| **High impact** | Real relevance, measurable impact, user research | Breakthrough tech, new algorithms, paradigm shift | Proven architecture, scalable design, clear roadmap | Advanced algorithms, performance, security focus | Compelling story, smooth demo, confident delivery |
| **Medium** | Problem complexity, market size, urgency | Better processes, improved UX, efficiency gains | Working prototype, known challenges | Clean code, good practices, documentation | Good slides, clear explanations, Q&A handling |
| **Low** | Basic statement, general cases | Minor tweaks, standard methods | Basic mockups, conceptual design | Basic functionality, simple methods | Basic presentation, adequate communication |
| **Mistakes** | Vague problems, no validation, unrealistic scope | Copying solutions, no differentiation | Unrealistic timeline, ignored constraints | Poor code quality, no testing, security gaps | Technical jargon, demo failures, poor timing |

**The one line worth keeping from this:** a deck excellent at three criteria and absent at two
scores worse than one solid at all five. The weakest slide is the one worth working on.

## 2. Slide-by-slide structure (varies with the official template)

### Slide 1, title page - mandatory
- Problem Statement **ID**, exact match from sih.gov.in
- Problem Statement **title**, exact wording from the official PS
- **Theme**
- **PS category**, software or hardware
- **Team ID**, as assigned by the SPOC
- **Team name** - unique, and it may **not** contain the college name
- **College name and location**

### Slide 2, proposed solution
- A clear, memorable solution **title**, not a restatement of the problem
- A direct **PS connection**: how it answers the exact requirement
- **Key innovation** highlighted - what makes it unique
- **Target beneficiaries** named
- **Impact metrics** - quantifiable improvement expected

### Slide 3, technical approach
- **Technology stack**, one or two lines, no buzzword pile
- **Architecture or process flow diagram** - a visual, not a list
- **Implementation methodology**, step by step
- **Data sources** named - APIs, datasets, sensors
- **Deployment strategy** - web, mobile, hardware

### Slide 4, feasibility and viability
- **Technical** feasibility - buildable with current technology
- **Resource** feasibility - the team can execute it
- **Market viability** - real demand exists
- **Scalability** - it can grow past a prototype
- **Risk mitigation** - how challenges get handled
- **Budget**, if applicable

### Slide 5, impact and benefits
- **Quantified impact** - specific numbers
- **Sustainability** - long-term benefit
- **Social and economic impact**
- **Competitive advantage** over alternatives
- **Future roadmap** beyond the hackathon

### Slide 6, research and references
- **Academic papers** - IEEE, ScienceDirect, Elsevier
- **Industry reports** supporting demand
- **Technology documentation** showing feasibility
- **Related work comparison** - how this differs

## 3. Content quality, by criterion

**Problem-solution alignment.** Every feature traced to a PS requirement. Addresses the root
cause, not symptoms. Measurable outcomes in a "reduces X by Y%" shape. User journey explained
end to end. Stakeholder impact articulated.

**Innovation and uniqueness.** A novel approach, not a copy. Creative use of technology. A clear
value proposition. Original insight. Any IP potential.

**Technical depth.** An architecture diagram. Realistic technology choices. A clear integration
strategy. Data flow in, processing, out. Performance considerations.

**Presentation quality.** Visual-first, diagrams over paragraphs. Clear language, no jargon pile.
Consistent design. Error-free. Logical flow between slides.

**Feasibility.** A realistic scope. Resources confirmed available. Team skills aligned. Risks
assessed. A clear MVP definition.

## 4. Elimination triggers

**Mechanical - checked before anybody reads a word**
- Wrong file format. Export to PDF.
- More than six slides, or the official template's structure modified.
- Missing mandatory elements: PS ID, team details.
- Late submission.
- More than two problem statements applied to.
- Team composition requirements unmet.
- No college or SPOC authorisation.

**Content**
- A generic solution not tailored to the specific PS.
- Unrealistic scope or promises.
- No research backing. "Trust us, it works."
- A solution looking for a problem.
- Buzzword overload - "AI ML IoT Blockchain" with no substance.
- Missing citations. Broken links.

**Presentation**
- Reading the deck word for word.
- Too long on the problem, too little on the solution.
- Team members contradicting each other.
- Jargon with no explanation.
- No connection between slides.

## 5. Portal fields, if the submission asks for them

**Idea title.** Be specific. Include the problem domain and the technology approach. Make it
memorable. Avoid generic titles, ALL CAPS, the college name, and buzzword spam.

**Idea description.** Suggested shape: problem analysis, proposed solution, technical
implementation, feasibility and impact, innovation and research.

**Abstract.** Introduction, problem statement, proposed solution, benefits and impact,
implementation plan, conclusion.

**Additional documents that help.** A research draft, a market or competitive analysis, detailed
architecture diagrams, prototype evidence, an impact assessment.

**Video.** 2 to 4 minutes. Problem, solution, demo, impact. Public or unlisted, never private.
Clear audio, HD.

## 6. What this project did NOT take from the above, and why

**[not applied] "36-hour deliverable scope" and the hour-by-hour hackathon plan.** That framing
is for a team that will build during the national round. This platform is already built,
deployed and public, which is a stronger position than any timeline. Slide 4 says "built,
deployed and public today" instead.

**[not applied] "Impact metrics: reduces X by Y%".** Tempting, and unavailable honestly - there
is no baseline measurement of how long an INCOIS forecaster currently takes, and inventing one
would be exactly the unbacked claim the same checklist warns about. Slide 5 uses figures that
are real and do not move instead.

**[not applied] "Intellectual property potential / patentable elements".** Nothing here is
patentable and claiming otherwise would not survive a question.

**[not applied] Quantified impact using any number the bake moves.** See `NOTES.md`. A slide
cannot be re-exported on the day a judge opens it, so instrument counts, test counts, source
counts and field counts stay off the slides. Stable figures are used freely: 5 m to 2000 m,
24 levels, 97%, 1991-2020, 110 km, "a tenth", zero network calls, the Montha bars.

**[partly applied] "Target beneficiaries specified" on slide 2.** They are on **slide 3**, in the
WHO USES IT row, and putting them on slide 2 as well was tried and reverted as a duplicate.

## 7. The competitor, for calibration

One rival team's deck for this same problem statement (SAGARDRISHTI, Team Sailors) is worth
looking at, not copying. See memory `competitor-landscape` for the wider survey.

**What theirs does better:** six numbered capability cards on slide 2, each a short bold title
and two plain sentences, with an icon. It reads as *capability* at a glance. A subtitle that is
a benefit in six words - "Turning Ocean Data into Safer Journeys". Project links in a box a
judge cannot miss.

**What theirs does worse, and what not to imitate:** four "innovation" cards of which two are
the same card duplicated; ML anomaly detection, crowdsourced observation validation and SOS
alerting, none of which has to work yet; a Cesium globe with flat slices rather than a volume
you enter.

**The conclusion drawn from it:** a competitor deck will out-promise this one every time,
because promises are free. The answer is not to match the promise count. It is to be the only
deck in the pile whose live link opens, and to state the capabilities as plainly as they state
theirs.
